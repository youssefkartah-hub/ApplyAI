01The job

A magnet, a 100 g package and an 80 g allowance

M.A.R.V. flies inside a motion-capture lab. Position and heading come from the lab's OptiTrack cameras, a Raspberry Pi 4 with a Navio2 board runs the flight software, and an MLX90640 thermal camera is the sensor payload. The mission adds one physical task: collect a 100 g package and carry it.

Four of us split the vehicle. My share answers two questions. How does the vehicle get hold of the package? And what does carrying it do to the way the vehicle flies?

  • 100 gPackage, with a steel plate on top for the magnet to hold.

02Decision 1 · October 2026

Let the landing do the work

The earlier pickup left 1.7 mm to the battery tray and 1.3 mm to the camera. A package that swings in flight needs more room than that, so the mechanism had to get slimmer and still come in under 80 g.

Passive or powered? Options I weighed

Passive ratchet Chosen for v4

  • No servo, no wiring and nothing to command.
  • Landing on the package does the lifting.
  • The vehicle has to land squarely on the package.
  • Travel is limited to the stroke of the tube.

Servo-driven pickup

  • Can reach down to a package from a hover.
  • A servo, a spool and wiring, inside the same 80 g.
  • Needs a way to put the magnet back in the same place every cycle.

Why passive first: it removes the most parts. If landing on the package could do the job, the servo's mass and wiring never had to enter the budget.

v4 is two tubes printed in PLA around a 30 mm N52 magnet. When the vehicle lands on the package, the package pushes the inner tube up through ten clicks of 3 mm, and pawls hold it at each click. A firm tug resets it.

03Decision 2 · November 2026

Spend a servo to buy reach

The ratchet has one hard requirement: the vehicle has to land squarely on the package, with 30 mm of stroke to work with. Picking up without landing means sending the magnet down to the package.

Keep the ratchet or build a winch? Options I weighed

v4 ratchet

  • 36.3 g and no actuator.
  • Only works on a square landing.
  • 30 mm of stroke.

v5 servo winch Chosen

  • 150 mm of reach, five times the ratchet's.
  • The puck is designed to seat itself in its dock.
  • Adds an FS90R servo, a spool and braided line.
  • One more thing to command in flight.

Why the winch: reach costs a servo, a spool and some way to return the magnet to the same place after every cycle. The seat was the part I cared about most, so the puck has a cone on top and winding it in pulls it into a matching conical dock.

An FS90R continuous-rotation servo turns the spool about 5.3 times to pay out 150 mm of braided line through a line guide. The right-hand drawing in Fig. 1.2 shows the puck at full reach, with the docked position dashed.

04Iteration · v5.1 to v5.2

0.2 mm in the wrong place

In v5.1 the magnet face sat only 0.2 mm below the rim of its printed housing. That is close enough to flush that the package's steel plate pressed on the plastic rim.

I wanted the magnet to carry the package and the print to carry nothing. In v5.2 I dropped the magnet to 3 mm below the rim, then checked the change against the team's Onshape frame model and the FS90R drawing.

The change bought clearance under the battery tray and cost something too: the rim no longer braces the package.

05Analysis · stability and control

What a 100 g package does to a 630 g vehicle

The package is 16% of the vehicle's mass, hung 45 mm ahead of centre and well below the rotors. One set of mass properties would hide what that does. I built the mass budget, pulled the CG and inertias from the CAD model for both cases, and presented them as the stability and control section of our preliminary design review.

What the numbers mean for the vehicle

  • The CG moves about 6 mm forward and 10 mm down. In a level hover the front rotors have to carry more of the weight than the rear pair.
  • Pitch and roll inertia rise 21% and 17%. The same control torque turns the loaded vehicle more slowly, so gains chosen for the empty vehicle will respond more sluggishly with the package aboard.
  • Yaw inertia barely moves. The package hangs close to the yaw axis, so it adds only 4%.

These values are for the v4 pickup. The winch has a different mass in a different place, so it needs its own pair of cases.

06A check that avoided a redesign

A strong magnet, 14 cm from a compass

The pickup magnet sits 13 to 15 cm from the Navio2's magnetometer. I estimated its field at the board at roughly 3 to 6 times Earth's {{ev estimate "Estimate"}}, far too much for a compass heading to survive.

Before changing anything I checked whether the vehicle depends on that sensor. It does not. Heading comes from motion capture, and the project leaves the magnetometer unused. A fix for a sensor nobody reads would have spent mass for nothing, so the pickup stayed where it was.

Limit of this conclusion

It holds for this vehicle in this lab. On an aircraft that steers by compass, the same pickup would need rework.

08What I took from it

Four things I will carry into the next design

  1. Put the numbers first.A mass budget and two clearances decided the v4 design before anything was printed.
  2. Check what a change touches.A magnet is a payload part until it sits 14 cm from a compass. Asking early turned a possible redesign into a note.
  3. Every fix costs something.Dropping the magnet 3 mm bought clearance and gave up the rim's support. Writing the trade down matters as much as making it.
  4. Say what is measured and what is estimated.The pawl forces are a model until a printed pair says otherwise, and the review reads that way.
