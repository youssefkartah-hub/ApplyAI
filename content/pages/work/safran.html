01The job

Test the part, and leave a record that proves it

From June to August 2024 I assisted in the production and testing of kerosene systems, including temperature probes and gauge devices, working with the production and test team on their performance and dependability, at Safran Aerosystems in Tiflet, Morocco.

The work ran on industry-standard procedures. A work instruction says how each test is run, a traveler follows each part, and a part that falls outside its specification is held until someone can show why.

03Three changes

What I saw, what I thought, what I did

Records that ate the clock

Every test ends with data entry: results into MATLAB and Excel logs, and fields on the traveler. Across about 110 tests on 6 part numbers that time adds up, and fields that differ from one log to the next invite mistakes. The test itself takes as long as it takes. The paperwork around it is the part that can be made the same every time.

I standardized the test logs and the traveler fields, so the same result goes in the same place for every part number. That saved about 3 minutes of data entry on every test, 5 to 8% of the test {{ev reported}}.

Tests that had to be run twice

Parts were collecting repeat-test and hold tags. Each one stops a part and costs a second pass on the bench. A change to the instruction reaches every operator and every later unit, which makes it the first place to look.

I worked with production staff to update the work instructions, the checklists and the margin-of-safety checks. The result was about 10 fewer repeat-test and hold tags {{ev reported}}.

A failed part and an open report

When a part fails, a nonconformance report stays open until someone can show what went wrong and why. The people who decide need the evidence in one place and quickly, while the part and the setup are still fresh.

I compiled root cause and corrective action data packs within 24 to 48 hours of a failure: test traces, as-built versus spec comparisons, and photos. The packs helped close 2 nonconformance reports {{ev reported}}.

05Looking back

Three months later

I wrote this reflection myself after the internship ended. Both halves are part of what it taught me.

From my own write-up

  • Hands-on technical workI gained hands-on experience in producing and testing sophisticated kerosene systems such as temperature probes and gauge devices.
  • Why standards are strictI learned how important it is to follow strict and precise procedures, and the consequences of not following them.
  • What a team really isThe team I worked with taught me what it means to be part of a group working toward one goal.
  • Document in more detailI took a good amount of notes on my day-to-day tasks. I should have taken more, and in far more detail.
  • Take on moreI asked for extra responsibilities from time to time. Asking more often would have taught me about things outside my circle.
  • Ask for feedbackI received feedback many times, but I went out of my way to ask my supervisor only three times. I should have done that far more.
