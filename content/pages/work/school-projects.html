01Measured · AERSP 305W structures lab · Fall 2026

Is carbon-fiber reinforcement worth its weight?

The question was whether bonding carbon fiber to an aluminum I-beam buys more stiffness than the weight it adds.

I measured the flexural rigidity of a plain beam and a reinforced one, five runs each, by two routes: from how far the beam deflects under load, and from the strain under the same loads.

A percentage means little without its uncertainty, so I carried the error through to the final ratio.

Do the two methods agree?

Not quite. The strain route reads lower, at +27 ± 2%. Its interval reaches 29%, and the deflection interval starts at 29.9%.

The headline number is the deflection result, which is the tighter of the two. I show the strain result beside it because the gap is information: something separates the two methods that the stated uncertainties do not cover.

Result {{ev measured}}

The reinforced beam is 30.5 ± 0.6% stiffer for 18.6% more weight, a 10.1 ± 0.5% gain in stiffness per pound. For a part sized by stiffness, the reinforcement pays for its weight.

02Calculated from CFD data · AERSP 424 · Python · Fall 2026

Forces on a transonic airfoil, from raw CFD output

The starting point was scattered viscous CFD output for a NACA 0012 at Mach 0.70 and 3.04° angle of attack. I did not run the simulation. The task was to get the forces out of the raw field and show they could be trusted.

Raw data comes with no answer key, so I computed lift by two independent routes: from the circulation around a contour enclosing the airfoil, and from the surface pressure. Drag came from two wake surveys. I also mapped the supersonic pocket on the upper surface, which ends in a shock near 30% of chord.

03Simulated · independent study · ANSYS Fluent · 2025

Less drag on an EV body, without starving the cooling

How much drag can come off an electric vehicle body without starving the battery and motor of cooling air?

I ran steady RANS simulations in ANSYS Fluent on 7 geometry variants that changed the body contours, the underbody and the wheel covers.

A drag change of a few percent can be the mesh talking. I checked every trend on 3 mesh densities before trusting it. That made 21 runs, so I scripted the post-processing in MATLAB, which took it from 10 minutes a run to 3.

I also studied boundary-layer behavior and the surface-finish assumptions in the model, to inform the choice of material and finish and the coating smoothness a design would need. The MATLAB scripts ran the parametric sweeps and organized the results with basic statistics.

Result {{ev simulated}}

About 12% lower drag coefficient, with the cooling flow kept. This is a prediction from simulation.

04Calculated · AERSP 424 · Python · with one teammate · Fall 2026

A root solver that knows when it has failed

At what speed is a 737-800's drag lowest, and at which two speeds is it 10% above that?

My teammate and I wrote a Newton-Raphson solver from scratch, with five stopping conditions, and ran it on the drag model at cruise altitude and at sea level.

The hard part was the bookkeeping. A solver that stops has not necessarily succeeded, so ours counts a run as a success only when the residual is actually met. We defended the work in a 15-minute oral exam.

05What I took from them

Four habits these projects built

  1. Get the answer twice.Lift from circulation and from pressure. Stiffness from deflection and from strain. Drag on three meshes. If two routes disagree, I have found something.
  2. A solver must know when it failed.Stopping is easy. Reporting honestly why it stopped is the part worth writing.
  3. Carry the uncertainty.30.5% is a claim. 30.5 ± 0.6% is a measurement.
  4. Automate the repeat work.Seven minutes saved per run is what made 21 runs reasonable.
