01The idea

Don't list your room. Sell your room.

Moving out means selling a dozen things in the last week of a lease. On a normal marketplace that is a dozen listings, a dozen chats and a dozen pickups.

Roomly makes the room the listing. The seller captures it once and approves what the AI drafts. Buyers browse the actual room, see what sits next to what, and can take several things in one trip.

The business idea, which I have not tested: students use it free, and a university pays per campus ($15K to $25K) as a waste-diversion program, because the furniture that sells is furniture that stays out of the dumpster.

05Decisions

Three choices that shaped it

A 3D room without a 3D scanner

A phone photo has no depth. A true reconstruction needs hardware or software most students will never have.

How does a buyer see the room? Options I weighed

Reconstruct it from photos

  • A faithful model of the real room.
  • Needs depth hardware or heavy software that students do not have.

Ask a vision model for layout Chosen

  • Works from ordinary phone photos.
  • Rooms are stored as positioned objects, the same shape a real reconstruction pipeline could fill in later.
  • It is a model of the room, not a scan.

Why: a buyer needs layout and scale: what is in the room, roughly where, and how big. A faithful scan adds little to that. I asked Claude vision for structured output (room dimensions, wall and floor colors, and each item with a 3D position) and built a dollhouse view from procedural furniture in React Three Fiber.

Let the AI draft, and the seller decide

The rule

A model can misread an item or price it badly, and if that went live on its own the seller would pay for the mistake. So each detected item shows how confident the model is, the seller taps an item to include it or change the price, and nothing publishes without the seller's approval.

Should Roomly take the money?

An investor pushed for in-app payments and shipping, which sounds like the obvious next feature. Before building it I wrote up both options with the fees on a real basket. Move the sliders to see what one sale leaves.

I argued for peer-to-peer handoff. On a $120 bundle Roomly would keep about $0.87. Collecting payment would also make it a marketplace facilitator for sales tax, and shipping furniture means owning freight damage.

Where the seller lives

A room listing is, by definition, a map to where someone sleeps. Buyers need distance to decide, and they need the door only after both sides agree to meet. Exact coordinates are rounded before they leave the server, a listing shows a nearby pickup landmark, and the address is shared privately in messages once buyer and seller connect.

06Shortcuts, taken on purpose

What is fake, and what replaces it

A solo build cannot do everything properly at once, and a demo that hides its shortcuts is a trap for whoever builds on it next. I took each shortcut where it is cheap to undo, and wrote each one down.

08What I took from it

Four things I took from building it

  1. Make the hard step disappear.Nobody wants to write twelve listings. The product is the capture pass that writes them.
  2. Let the AI draft and the person decide.The model is fast and sometimes wrong. Approval is cheap and keeps the seller in charge.
  3. Run the numbers before the feature.Eighty-seven cents on a $120 sale settled an argument that opinions could not.
  4. Name the shortcuts.An MVP is honest when its README says what is fake.
